\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Community detection consists in finding clusters in a graph where nodes share more connections with nodes of the same cluster
than with nodes of other clusters. This broad topic is detailed in \citep{fortunato2010community}. In this 
report, we focus on random geometric graphs (RGG). Adding spatial information allows us to consider
local information based on real or abstract proximity.  % The probability of connection between two nodes depends on their distance.
In social networks, two individuals are more likely to be friends if they are geographically close,
or if they share similar interests, age, etc.
More concrete examples of RGG can be found in wireless networks \citep{gomez2015case}, in biology \citep{higham2008fitting},
and also in community detection problems \citep{sankararaman2020comhapdet}
\textcolor{red}{ word embedding, physic, ...}.

The main reference model for community detection is the Stochastic Block Model (SBM)
\citep{holland1983stochastic}. Each node carries a hidden community label and edge probabilities depend on whether two nodes belong to the same community or not.
This model comes from the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph, where each node connects to others with the same probability \citep{erdHos1960evolution}. This model is now well understood,
see \citep{abbe2018community}. Nonetheless, it has some drawbacks, since it is unrealistic to think that every node can connect to every other one with the same probability scale.
Around the same time as Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi, the RGG was introduced by \citep{gilbert1961random}.
Adding geometric information allows us to mimic some emergent behaviours of real networks,
such as sparsity \citep{Del_Genio_2011}, the "small-world" property \citep{watts1998collective}
or transitivity, namely the idea that a friend of a friend is more likely to be a friend.


RGG and its properties are well studied, see \citep{penrose2003random} and more recently \citep{duchemin2023random}.
When we add community structure, we are in a different setting.
We observe the graph, and sometimes also the node locations,
while the community labels remain hidden. 
The natural question is then whether the latent structure can be detected, and if so, how accurately the labels can be recovered.
We are interested in several levels of inference.
First, \textit{distinguishability} asks whether the observed graph comes from a geometric model with communities or from a null model without community structure. This is a testing problem. It aims to distinguish the presence of a community structure, not to recover the labels. Then, if recovery is possible, one can ask how accurate it can be.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Weak/partial recovery}: detecting communities better than random guessing
    \item \textit{Almost exact recovery}: the fraction of misclassified vertices vanishes as the number of nodes grows.
    \item \textit{Exact recovery}: all community labels are correctly recovered, up to permutation.
\end{itemize}
Thus, in this report, community detection is not only understood as
producing a convenient partition of the observed graph, but rather as
recovering latent community labels under a probabilistic geometric model.
% up to permutation.


Previously, we emphasized properties that RGG share with real networks due
to the geometric information. However,
one can also obtain transitivity, and surely more, with attribute-based models
\citep{Li_Sha_Huang_Zhang_2018}, 
hypergraphs \citep{Ruggeri_2023} and even geometric hypergraphs
\citep{Kov_cs_2025}.
We will not consider these models here, in order to keep the focus on the geometric aspect.
Moreover, among the problems mentioned above, one is \textit{Distinguishability} which should not be confused with the \textit{Detection Problem} that we do not study here.
The latter usually asks for conditions on the parameters defining the RGG under which the model is distinguishable from 
an Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph. Hence, this is not directly a community question. See,
\citep{bubeck2016testing}
\citep{liu2022testing}
\citep{mao2026randomgeometricgraphssmooth}.
Finally, at the beginning of the literature,
some works were interested in recovering the latent position of nodes in the geometric space,
and considered this as a community detection problem, \citep{valdivia2019latent}
\citep{eldan2022community}.
This is a different problem from the one we are interested in,
and it's in some sense harder, because one wants to recover the position (a continuous label) of each node rather than a community (a discrete label).

\section{Problem setting and inference tasks}\label{sec:setting}

Formally, we observe a graph \(G \coloneqq (V,E)\), where \(V\) is the vertex set and \(E\) is the set of edges between vertices. Throughout the report, \(n\) denotes the asymptotic scaling parameter. Depending on the model, the observed number of vertices may be either deterministic or random, but is of order \(n\). We consider a simple undirected graph \textcolor{red}{Une remarque là dessus ? Pourquoi non-orienté est classique comme hypothese ?}, without self-loops. The vertices are partitioned into \(k\) communities \(C_1,\dots,C_k\), so
\(V=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^k C_i\). We denote by \(\sigma^\star:V\to [k]\) the ground-truth community label function, defined by \(\sigma^\star(v)=i\) whenever \(v\in C_i\). The community structure is hidden, and our goal is to recover it from the observed data.

An estimator is therefore a function of the observations. Depending on the model, the observations may consist only of the graph \(G\), or of the graph together with the node locations \(X=(X_v)_{v\in V}\). Accordingly, we write the estimator as \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G)\) or \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G,X)\), and we view \(\hat{\sigma}:V\to [k]\) as an estimated labeling.
In general, the labels are only identifiable up to a relabeling of the communities. Indeed, permuting the names of the communities does not change the underlying partition of the vertex set. For this reason, the quality of an estimator is measured up to permutation. Denoting by \(\mathfrak{S}_k\) the symmetric group on \([k]\), we define
\[
A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)
\coloneqq \frac{1}{|V|}
\max_{\pi\in \mathfrak{S}_k}
\sum_{u\in V}
\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\hat{\sigma}(u)=\pi(\sigma^\star(u))\bigr\}
\]
Thus, \(A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)\) measures the fraction of correctly classified vertices after the best possible relabeling of the communities. 
We now give more formal definitions of the problems introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}.
\paragraph{Distinguishability.}
% This is a hypothesis testing problem introduced in \citep{abbe2021community}. Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of probability distributions corresponding to a geometric model with communities, and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of null distributions without community structure. A test is a measurable map
% \(\varphi_n=\varphi_n(G,X)\in\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model. We say that \(\mathbb P_n\) and \(\mathbb Q_n\) are distinguishable if
% error of type I and type II errors tends to \(0\), i.e. there exists a sequence of tests
% \((\varphi_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that
% \[
% \mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \qquad\text{and}\qquad
% \mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \]
Following \citep{sankararaman2018community}, distinguishability is a hypothesis testing problem.
Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a geometric random graph model with community structure and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a null geometric model without communities, both defined
on the same observation space. Let \(\Omega_n\) be the observation space. A test is a measurable function \(\varphi_n: \Omega_n \to\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model.
We say that \((\mathbb P_n)\) and \((\mathbb Q_n)\) are distinguishable if there exist tests
\((\varphi_n)_{n\ge 1}\) and a constant \(\gamma>0\) such that, under the uniform prior on
\(\{\mathbb P_n,\mathbb Q_n\}\),
\[
\mathbb P\big(\text{\(\varphi_n\) makes the correct decision}\big)
= \frac12\,\mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)+\frac12\,\mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)
\ge \frac12+\gamma-o_n(1)
\]

\paragraph{Weak/Partial recovery.}
We say that weak/partial recovery is achievable if the estimator performs better than random guessing, that's to say in the symmetric case, if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) and a constant \(\alpha>1/k\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq \alpha\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Almost exact recovery.}
We say that almost exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that for every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq 1-\varepsilon\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Exact recovery.}
We say that exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)=1\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]

Most works in the literature focus on the case \(k=2\). Although several ideas extend beyond this setting, this is also the framework that we adopt in the following, except when specified otherwise. In that case, it is convenient to encode the labels by \(\{-1,+1\}\), instead of \([2] = \{1,2\}\). Then “up to permutation” reduces to a global sign flip.

Each vertex also belongs to a space \(\mathcal X\). In the following, we specify each time whether this space is latent or observed. For theoretical analysis, \(\mathcal{X}\) is often chosen for mathematical convenience. The Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\) is a natural reference because of its symmetry, homogeneity, isotropy, and direct geometric interpretation. See \citep{abbe2021community}, \textcolor{red}{Citer ceux qui sont euclidiens}. \textcolor{red}{In the following, \(d\) is an integer.}
We also encounter the torus, mainly to avoid boundary effects and to focus on the core ideas of the proofs, \textcolor{red}{Todo citer}. Lastly, for latent models, the high-dimensional unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) is a standard reference space to model similarity, and it also provides convenient spectral properties \citep{decastro2020adaptiveestimationnonparametricgeometric}. A summary of theoretical works on \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) is given in \citep{duchemin2023random}.

In Section~\ref{sec:models} we present models used throughout the literature. Hence, we suggest a model to unify them while remaining close to the notation used in the papers. We call it the \textit{Unified Geometric Community Model} (UGCM) and it is defined as,
\[
\mathrm{UGCM} \left( \mathcal X, \rho, W_n, \mu_n, Z, \pi, \mathcal Y, K_n \right)
\] 
where \( \mathcal X \) is the ambient space and \(\rho\) is its metric. \(W_n \subset \mathcal X\) is the window where nodes are sampled at the scale $n$.  
\(\mu_n\) governs the spatial sampling mechanism. Usually, the vertices are sampled either as \(n\) i.i.d. points in \(W_n\) with law \(\mu_n\) or as a Poisson Point Process (PPP) on \(W_n\)  with intensity measure \( \mu_n \). We denote by \(Z\) the label set, and by \(\pi=(\pi_i)_{i\in Z} \) the distribution of the labels.
For all \(u \in V\) and independently, \( \sigma^\star(u) \sim \pi\). \(\mathcal Y\) the space of pairwise observation. Let \(\mathcal P(\mathcal Y)\) denote the set of probability distributions on \(\mathcal Y\), we consider the kernel observation,
\[
K_n : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \times Z \times Z \to \mathcal P(\mathcal Y)
\]
and for each unordered pair of vertices \(u,v\), we observe, 
\[
Y_{uv}\mid (X,\sigma^\star)\sim K_n\bigl(X_u,X_v,\sigma^\star(u),\sigma^\star(v)\bigr)
\]
The kernel is the core of the model. We note that Soft-RGG introduced by \citep{Penrose_2016} and developed in \citep{duchemin2023random} can be seen as a special case of UGCM where there exists a function \(f_n : \mathbb{R}^+ \to [0,1]\) such that for every \(x,y\in\mathcal{X}\) and \(z,z'\in Z\), \(K_n(x,y,z,z') = \text{Ber}(f_n(\rho(x,y))) \). Moreover, a particular case of Soft-RGG are the usual random geometric graph, i.e. the hard-threshold special case, when \(f_n\) is an indicator function, i.e. there exists \(r_n>0\) such that  \(K_n(x,y,z,z') = \delta_{\mathbf{1}\{\rho(x,y)\le r_n\}}\). Then, this framework also accommodates weighted graphs, since the observation space \(\mathcal Y\) is not necessarily binary.

As we will see, several phase-transition phenomena depend on the connectivity scale of the graph, which is usually measured through the average degree. Let \(D_n\) denote the degree of a vertex chosen uniformly at random. We can distinguish three regimes of connectivity, depending on the growth of \(\mathbb E(D_n) \) as \(n\to\infty\).
The most realistic but also the most delicate regime is the \emph{sparse regime}. Each vertex only carries a finite amount of local information, the expected degree remains bounded,
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(1)
\]
In the \emph{logarithmic regime}, the graph becomes sufficiently connected for exact recovery to become meaningful, 
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(\log n)
\]
Finally, the \emph{dense regime} corresponds to the easiest setting, the graph carries a lot of information,
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(n)
\]

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Geometric Stochastic Block Model (GSBM)}}
\citep{sankararaman2018community} \citep{abbe2021community}



The \emph{Geometric Stochastic Block Model} (GSBM), introduced in \citep{sankararaman2018community} and further developed in \citep{abbe2018community},
extends the classical SBM by incorporating a latent geometric structure. The vertices are embedded in a Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), typically according to a Poisson point process, and each vertex is assigned a 
hidden label \( \sigma^\star(u)\in\{-1,+1\} \). An edge between \(u\) and \(v\) is then drawn with a probability that depends both on their distance \( r=\|X_u-X_v\|_2 \) and on their labels: one typically 
writes \( p_{\mathrm{in}}^{(n)}(r) \) if \( \sigma^\star(u)=\sigma^\star(v) \), and \( p_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}(r) \) otherwise, with in general \( p_{\mathrm{in}}^{(n)}(r)>p_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}(r) \).
 So the model can be naturally written in the UGCM framework as
\[
\mathrm{GSBM} (d,r)
=
\mathrm{UGCM}\!\left(\mathbb{R}^d,\|\cdot\|_2,W_n,\mu_n,\{-1,+1\},\mathrm{Ber}(1/2),\{0,1\},K_n\right)
\]
Here, \(W_n \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) is the observation window and \(\mu_n=\lambda \mathbf{1}_{W_n}(x)\,dx\) is the intensity measure of a Poisson point process on \(W_n\). The observation kernel is Bernoulli and depends both on geometry and on the latent labels:
\[
K_n(x,y,z,z')
=
\mathrm{Ber}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{in}}^{(n)}(\|x-y\|)\mathbf 1_{\{z=z'\}}
+
p_{\mathrm{out}}^{(n)}(\|x-y\|)\mathbf 1_{\{z\neq z'\}}
\right)
\]

The foundational works of Sankararaman \citep{sankararaman2018community} and \citep{abbe2021community} show that geometry profoundly alters the recovery thresholds. In the sparse regime,
\(\mathbb{E}[D_n]=\Theta(1)\), each vertex carries only a finite amount of local information, which makes partial recovery difficult. In the logarithmic regime,
\(\mathbb{E}[D_n]=\Theta(\log n)\), exact recovery becomes the natural objective. In this setting, they obtain the necessary condition
\[
\lambda \nu_d\!\left(1-\sqrt{ab}-\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}\right)<1
\]
where \(\lambda\) is the intensity of the Poisson point process, \(\nu_d\) is the volume of the unit ball in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and \(a,b\) are the within- and between-community connection parameters in the simple threshold model. 
They also introduce the \emph{Good-Bad-Grid} algorithm, which partitions the space into cells, identifies sufficiently regular regions, and then propagates community information locally. 

More recently,\citep{gaudio2024exactcommunityrecoverygeometric}  showed that this threshold can actually be achieved by a linear-time algorithm built in two stages: a local propagation step producing an almost-exact labeling, 
followed by a vertex-wise refinement based on a Poisson test. 
Finally, \citep{gaudio2024exactcommunityrecoverygeometric} extended this perspective to more general Euclidean random graphs, in which the GSBM appears as a special case, and expressed the exact recovery threshold in terms of a Chernoff--Hellinger divergence.
