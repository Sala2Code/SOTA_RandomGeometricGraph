\begin{abstract}
This report surveys recent theoretical advances on community detection in geometric random graph models. These models incorporate spatial or latent geometric information and therefore generate strong local dependence, high triangle density, and transitivity. We review the main geometric community models studied in the literature, including the Geometric Block Model, the Geometric Stochastic Block Model, the Geometric Kernel Block Model, the Geometric Hidden Community Model and the Soft Geometric Block Model. We organize the literature by inference task and connectivity regime, with emphasis on weak, almost exact, and exact recovery, information-theoretic thresholds, and the algorithmic ideas used to achieve them. Particular attention is paid to the logarithmic degree regime, where recent works establish sharp exact-recovery thresholds together with efficient algorithms. We also discuss spectral approaches, extensions and open directions.
\keywords{Community detection, geometric random graphs, spatial networks, exact recovery, partial recovery, information-theoretic thresholds, spectral methods}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Community detection consists in finding clusters in a graph where nodes share more connections with nodes of the same cluster
than with nodes of other clusters. This broad topic is detailed in \citep{fortunato2010community}. In this 
report, we focus on random geometric graphs (RGG). Adding spatial information allows us to consider
local information based on real or abstract proximity.  % The probability of connection between two nodes depends on their distance.
In social networks, two individuals are more likely to be friends if they are geographically close,
or if they share similar interests, age, etc.
More concrete examples of RGG can be found in wireless networks \citep{gomez2015case}, in biology \citep{higham2008fitting},
and also in community detection problems \citep{sankararaman2020comhapdet}.

The main reference model for community detection is the Stochastic Block Model (SBM)
\citep{holland1983stochastic}. Each node carries a hidden community label and edge probabilities depend on whether two nodes belong to the same community or not.
This model comes from the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph, where each node connects to others with the same probability \citep{erdHos1960evolution}. This model is now well understood,
see \citep{abbe2018community}. Nonetheless, it has some drawbacks, since it is unrealistic to think that every node can connect to every other one with the same probability scale.
Around the same time as Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi, the RGG was introduced by \citep{gilbert1961random}.
Adding geometric information allows us to mimic some emergent behaviours of real networks,
such as sparsity \citep{Del_Genio_2011},
%the "small-world" property \citep{watts1998collective} % c'est pas du tout le cas pour les grpahes géométriques considérés !! (à ajouter pour hypeorblic dans Ouverture)
or transitivity, namely the idea that a friend of a friend is more likely to be a friend.


RGG and its properties are well studied, see \citep{penrose2003random} and more recently \citep{duchemin2023random}.
When we add community structure, we are in a different setting.
We observe the graph, and sometimes also the node locations,
while the community labels remain hidden. 
The natural question is then whether the latent structure can be detected, and if so, how accurately the labels can be recovered.
We are interested in several levels of inference.
First, \textit{distinguishability} asks whether the observed graph comes from a geometric model with communities or from a null model without community structure. This is a testing problem. It aims to distinguish the presence of a community structure, not to recover the labels. Then, if recovery is possible, one can ask how accurate it can be.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Weak/partial recovery}: detecting communities better than random guessing
    \item \textit{Almost exact recovery}: the fraction of misclassified vertices vanishes as the number of nodes grows.
    \item \textit{Exact recovery}: all community labels are correctly recovered, up to permutation.
\end{itemize}
Thus, in this report, community detection is not only understood as
producing a convenient partition of the observed graph, but rather as
recovering latent community labels under a probabilistic geometric model.
% up to permutation.


Previously, we emphasized properties that RGG share with real networks due
to the geometric information. However,
one can also obtain transitivity, and surely more, with attribute-based models
\citep{Li_Sha_Huang_Zhang_2018}, 
hypergraphs \citep{Ruggeri_2023} and even geometric hypergraphs
\citep{Kov_cs_2025}.
We will not consider these models here, in order to keep the focus on the geometric aspect. A related but distinct line of work considers mixture based latent position models, such as the Gaussian Mixture Block Model
\citep{li2024spectralclusteringgaussianmixture}, where community labels determine node positions using Gaussian distributions. See \citep{reynolds2009gaussian} \citep{NIPS1999_97d98119}.
This differs from the geometric models considered here, where geometry directly affects edge formation rather than determining node placement. We therefore don't include these models in the core of this survey.


Moreover, among the problems mentioned above, one is \textit{Distinguishability} which should not be confused with the \textit{Detection Problem} that we do not study here.
The latter usually asks for conditions on the parameters defining the RGG under which the model is distinguishable from 
an Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph. Hence, this is not directly a community question. See,
\citep{bubeck2016testing}
\citep{liu2022testing}
\citep{mao2026randomgeometricgraphssmooth}.
Finally, at the beginning of the literature,
some works were interested in recovering the latent position of nodes in the geometric space,
and considered this as a community detection problem, \citep{valdivia2019latent}
\citep{eldan2022community}.
This is a different problem from the one we are interested in,
and it's in some sense harder, because one wants to recover the position (a continuous label) of each node rather than a community (a discrete label).

\section{Problem setting and inference tasks}\label{sec:setting}

Formally, we observe a graph \(G \coloneqq (V,E)\), where \(V\) is the vertex set and \(E\) is the set of edges between vertices. Throughout the report, \(n\) denotes the asymptotic scaling parameter. Depending on the model, the observed number of vertices may be either deterministic or random, but is of order \(n\). We consider a simple undirected graph, without self-loops. The vertices are partitioned into \(k\) communities \(C_1,\dots,C_k\), so
\(V=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^k C_i\). We denote by \(\sigma^\star:V\to [k]\) the ground-truth community label function, defined by \(\sigma^\star(v)=i\) whenever \(v\in C_i\). The community structure is hidden, and our goal is to recover it from the observed data.

An estimator is therefore a function of the observations. Depending on the model, the observations may consist only of the graph \(G\), or of the graph together with the node locations \(X=(X_v)_{v\in V}\). Accordingly, we write the estimator as \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G)\) or \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G,X)\), and we view \(\hat{\sigma}:V\to [k]\) as an estimated labeling.
In general, the labels are only identifiable up to a relabeling of the communities. Indeed, permuting the names of the communities does not change the underlying partition of the vertex set. For this reason, the quality of an estimator is measured up to permutation. Denoting by \(\mathfrak{S}_k\) the symmetric group on \([k]\), we define
\[
A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)
\coloneqq \frac{1}{|V|}
\max_{\pi\in \mathfrak{S}_k}
\sum_{u\in V}
\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\hat{\sigma}(u)=\pi(\sigma^\star(u))\bigr\}
\]
Thus, \(A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)\) measures the fraction of correctly classified vertices after the best possible relabeling of the communities. 
We now give more formal definitions of the problems introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}.
\paragraph{Distinguishability.}
% This is a hypothesis testing problem introduced in \citep{abbe2021community}. Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of probability distributions corresponding to a geometric model with communities, and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of null distributions without community structure. A test is a measurable map
% \(\varphi_n=\varphi_n(G,X)\in\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model. We say that \(\mathbb P_n\) and \(\mathbb Q_n\) are distinguishable if
% error of type I and type II errors tends to \(0\), i.e. there exists a sequence of tests
% \((\varphi_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that
% \[
% \mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \qquad\text{and}\qquad
% \mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \]
Following \citep{sankararaman2018community}, distinguishability is a hypothesis testing problem.
Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a geometric random graph model with community structure and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a null geometric model without communities, both defined
on the same observation space. Let \(\Omega_n\) be the observation space. A test is a measurable function \(\varphi_n: \Omega_n \to\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model.
We say that \((\mathbb P_n)\) and \((\mathbb Q_n)\) are distinguishable if there exist tests
\((\varphi_n)_{n\ge 1}\) and a constant \(\gamma>0\) such that, under the uniform prior on
\(\{\mathbb P_n,\mathbb Q_n\}\),
\[
\mathbb P\big(\text{\(\varphi_n\) makes the correct decision}\big)
= \frac12\,\mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)+\frac12\,\mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)
\ge \frac12+\gamma-o_n(1)
\]

\paragraph{Weak/Partial recovery.}
We say that weak/partial recovery is achievable if the estimator performs better than random guessing, that's to say in the symmetric case, if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) and a constant \(\alpha>1/k\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq \alpha\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Almost exact recovery.}
We say that almost exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that for every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq 1-\varepsilon\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Exact recovery.}
We say that exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)=1\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]

Most works in the literature focus on the case \(k=2\). Although several ideas extend beyond this setting, this is also the framework that we adopt in the following, except when specified otherwise. In that case, it is convenient to encode the labels by \(\{\pm1\}=\{-1,+1\}\), instead of \([2] = \{1,2\}\). Then “up to permutation” reduces to a global sign flip.

Each vertex also belongs to a space \(\mathcal X\). In the following, we specify each time whether this space is latent or observed. For theoretical analysis, \(\mathcal{X}\) is often chosen for mathematical convenience. The Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is a natural reference because of its symmetry, homogeneity, isotropy, and direct geometric interpretation.
% See \citep{abbe2021community}, \textcolor{red}{Citer ceux qui sont euclidiens}.
In the following, \(d\) is an integer.
We also encounter the torus \(\mathbb T^d\) mainly to avoid boundary effects and to focus on the core ideas of the proofs. 
%\textcolor{red}{Todo citer}.
Lastly, for latent models, the high-dimensional unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d}\) is a standard reference space to model similarity, and it also provides convenient spectral properties \citep{decastro2020adaptiveestimationnonparametricgeometric}. A summary of theoretical works on \(\mathbb{S}^{d}\) is given in \citep{duchemin2023random}.

In Section~\ref{sec:models} we present models used throughout the literature. To unify notation, we use the following template while remaining close to the notation used in the papers. We call it the \textit{Unified Geometric Community Model} (UGCM) and it is defined as,
\[
\mathrm{UGCM} \left( \mathcal X, \rho, W_n, \mu_n, Z, \pi, \mathcal Y, K_n \right)
\] 
where \( \mathcal X \) is the ambient space and \(\rho\) is its metric. \(W_n \subset \mathcal X\) is the window where nodes are sampled at the scale $n$.  
\(\mu_n\) governs the spatial sampling mechanism. Usually, the vertices are sampled either as \(n\) i.i.d. points in \(W_n\) with law \(\mu_n\) or as a Poisson Point Process (PPP) on \(W_n\)  with intensity measure \( \mu_n \). We denote by \(Z\) the label set, and by \(\pi=(\pi_i)_{i\in Z} \) the distribution of the labels.
For all \(u \in V\) and independently, \( \sigma^\star(u) \sim \pi\). \(\mathcal Y\) is the space of pairwise observation. Let \(\mathcal P(\mathcal Y)\) denote the set of probability distributions on \(\mathcal Y\), we consider the observation kernel,
\[
K_n : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \times Z \times Z \to \mathcal P(\mathcal Y)
\]
and for each unordered pair of vertices \(u,v\), we observe, 
\[
Y_{uv}\mid (X,\sigma^\star)\sim K_n\bigl(X_u,X_v,\sigma^\star(u),\sigma^\star(v)\bigr)
\]
The kernel is the core of the model. We note that Soft-RGG introduced by \citep{Penrose_2016} and developed in \citep{duchemin2023random} can be seen as a special case of UGCM where there exists a function \(f_n : \mathbb{R}^+ \to [0,1]\) such that for every \(x,y\in\mathcal{X}\) and \(z,z'\in Z\), \(K_n(x,y,z,z') = \text{Ber}(f_n(\rho(x,y))) \). Moreover, a particular case of Soft-RGG are the usual random geometric graph, i.e. the hard-threshold special case, when \(f_n\) is an indicator function, i.e. there exists \(r_n>0\) such that  \(K_n(x,y,z,z') = \delta_{\mathbf{1}\{\rho(x,y)\le r_n\}}\). Then, this framework also accommodates weighted graphs, since the observation space \(\mathcal Y\) is not necessarily binary.

As we will see, several phase-transition phenomena depend on the connectivity scale of the graph, which is usually measured through the average degree. Let \(D_n\) denote the degree of a vertex chosen uniformly at random. We can distinguish three regimes of connectivity, depending on the growth of \(\mathbb E(D_n) \) as \(n\to\infty\).
The most realistic but also the most delicate regime is the \emph{sparse regime}. Each vertex only carries a finite amount of local information, the expected degree remains bounded,
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(1)
\]
In the \emph{logarithmic regime}, the graph becomes sufficiently connected for exact recovery to become meaningful, 
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(\log n)
\]
Finally, the \emph{dense regime} corresponds to the easiest setting, the graph carries a lot of information,
\[
\mathbb E(D_n)=\Theta(n)
\] 


\section{Geometric community models and recovery thresholds}\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Geometric Block Model (GBM)}

One of the earliest geometric models for community detection is the Geometric Block Model (GBM) introduced in \citep{galhotra2018geometric}. They mainly study the case of two symmetric communities. In its general form, the model places each vertex at a latent position on the unit sphere \(\mathcal X \coloneqq \mathbb S^{d-1}\), sampled uniformly, and edges are drawn through a hard geometric rule. For \(d \in \mathbb{N} \backslash \{0,1\}, \ r_\mathrm{in}, r_\mathrm{out} \in [-1,1]\), \(r_{ij} \coloneqq  r_\mathrm{in}\delta_{ij} + r_\mathrm{out}(1-\delta_{ij}) \), they define
\[
\mathrm{GBM}(r_\mathrm{in}, r_\mathrm{out}, d) \coloneqq \mathrm{UGCM}(\mathcal X,\|\cdot\|_2,\ \mathcal X ,  \mathrm{Unif}( \mathcal X ),  \{\pm1\},  \text{Ber}(1/2),  \{0,1\}, K_n)
\]
with \(K_n : (x,y,z,z') \mapsto \mathbf{1}\{\langle x, y \rangle \geq r_{z,z'}\} \).
% In the circular case, this is equivalent after reparametrization to a threshold rule based either on the Euclidean distance or on the geodesic distance.
The condition \(r_\mathrm{in}>r_\mathrm{out}\) ensures that edges within communities are denser than across communities. The space \(\mathcal X\) is assumed latent, so recovery is done only from the adjacency matrix.

They study the one dimensional circular case, corresponding to \(\mathbb S^1 \subset \mathbb R^2\), hence \(d=2\) in the notation above, in the regime
\[
r_\mathrm{in} \coloneqq a\frac{\log n}{n} \qquad r_\mathrm{out} \coloneqq b\frac{\log n}{n} \qquad a>b>0
\]
This corresponds to the logarithmic regime. This regime matches the connectivity scale of one dimensional random geometric graphs. Their main algorithm is motif based, that is, for an observed edge \((u,v) \in E\), they count the number of common neighbors of \(u\) and \(v\), so the number of triangles containing this edge. Then they use this statistic to decide whether \(u\) and \(v\) belong to the same community. The geometry creates strong local dependence and transitivity. Unlike in the SBM, triangles are informative in the GBM. Still in this regime, the simple motif counting procedure already yields exact recovery with high probability when \(r_\mathrm{in}\ge 4r_\mathrm{out}\). The algorithm is efficient, and the journal version later clarifies that this one threshold guarantee from the conference paper is order optimal up to constants but restricted to that parameter range \citep{galhotra2018geometric,galhotra2023communityrecoverygeometricblock}.

Firstly, \citep{galhotra2020connectivityrandomannulusgraphs} introduces the random annulus graph (RAG). Let \(I \coloneqq \left[b\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}, a\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right] \). They study \( RAG_d(n, I)\) on \(\mathbb S^d \), a RGG where two nodes are connected only when the distance between them falls inside \(I\), hence the name annulus graph. In dimension \(1\), they obtain a sharp connectivity threshold for the RAG: the graph is connected with high probability if \(a>1\) and \(a-b>1/2\), and disconnected with high probability if \(a<1\) or \(a-b<1/2\). For \(d > 1\), they provide sufficient conditions \citep{galhotra2020connectivityrandomannulusgraphs}.

Secondly, the main algorithmic refinement is to replace the original one threshold triangle rule by a two threshold procedure. Once one leaves the regime \(r_\mathrm{in}\ge 4r_\mathrm{out}\), a single threshold is no longer sufficient. For inter community edges, the normalized triangle count is concentrated around \(2r_\mathrm{out}\), whereas for intra community edges it depends on the latent distance and may lie either above or below that value. They therefore define two thresholds
\[
E_S\coloneqq (2b+f_1)\frac{\log n}{n}
\qquad
E_D\coloneqq (2b-f_2)\frac{\log n}{n}
\]
where \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are chosen from Chernoff type large deviation inequalities. Edges whose triangle count falls inside the ambiguous interval \([E_D,E_S]\) are removed. This removes all inter community edges with high probability, while preserving enough informative intra community edges. Then, RAG connectivity results are used to show that the surviving intra community edges still connect each community.

As a consequence, they obtain exact recovery in dimension \(1\) with explicit conditions and an impossibility result when \(a<1\) or \(a-b<1/2\). However, the exact recovery threshold for the latent one dimensional GBM is still not fully characterized, see Figure~\ref{fig:GBM}. A useful contrast is that this gap disappears when the latent positions are revealed. In that case, the one dimensional GBM has a sharp exact recovery threshold which coincides with the sharp connectivity threshold of the RAG. In higher dimension \(d>1\), they also prove a polynomial time exact recovery result at the correct order of magnitude,
\[
r_\mathrm{out}=\Theta\!\left(\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right)
\quad\text{and}\quad
r_\mathrm{in}-r_\mathrm{out}=\Theta\!\left(\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}\right)
\]
are sufficient, while recovery becomes impossible if one of these quantities is of smaller order \citep{galhotra2023communityrecoverygeometricblock}.

Finally, \citep{chien2020active} studies active learning in the latent GBM with two communities. The model is the same, but the learner is now allowed to query the labels of a small number of carefully chosen vertices to a perfect oracle. Their approach keeps the motif counting idea as a first step, in order to remove many inter community edges, and then uses label queries in a second step. They propose two algorithms. The first combines this pruning step with the graph based active learning procedure \(S^2\) \citep{dasarathy2015s2efficientgraphbased}, while the second is more aggressive and then queries one label per connected component. Their main result is that exact recovery becomes possible with high probability using only a sublinear number of queries, even in parameter regimes where the best known unsupervised guarantees fail. This gives a smooth trade off between the hardness of clustering and the query complexity. They do not prove any optimality result for this active setting. Moreover, the theoretical bounds seem somewhat pessimistic. Although the analysis favors the first algorithm, the paper notes that the second often uses fewer queries in practice, which suggests that the proof can likely be improved \citep{chien2020active}.


\subsection{Geometric Stochastic Block Model (GSBM)}

\citep{sankararaman2018community} introduce the Geometric Stochastic Block Model (GSBM), which is later developed in full detail in \citep{abbe2021community}. 
The geometric positions are observed, the space \(\mathcal X\coloneqq \mathbb R^d\) is not latent.
The model allows edge probabilities to depend jointly on distance and community labels.
Here, \(n\) is a scaling parameter and not the number of nodes. Points are sampled as a PPP on a window \(W_n \coloneqq \left[-\frac{n^{1/d}}{2}, \frac{n^{1/d}}{2}\right]^d\) with intensity measure \(\mu_n \coloneqq \lambda \mathrm{Leb}_{\mid W_n}\) with \(\lambda > 0\). The window \(W_n\) has a volume \(n\). They study the case of two symmetric communities. Nodes are connected according to their distance and labels through \(f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}\) and \(f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}\). We assume for all \(r\geq 0\), \(1 \geq f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}(r) \geq f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}(r) \geq 0 \) and \(f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)} \neq f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}\) up to Lebesgue measure \(0\). More assumptions on these functions are given according to the regime that we are interested. % We are in the framework of Soft-RGG. 
The model is then defined by,
\[
\mathrm{GSBM}(f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}, f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)},\lambda,d) \coloneqq 
\mathrm{UGCM}(\mathcal X, \rho_n, W_n, \mu_n, \{\pm1\}, \text{Ber}(1/2), \{0,1\}, K_n)
\]
where the metric is the Euclidean distance in the sparse regime, \(\rho_n \coloneqq \| \cdot \|_2 \), and toroidal metric in the logarithmic regime, \(\rho_n \coloneqq \| \cdot \|_{T_n}\). The toroidal metric is used in the logarithmic regime to avoid boundary effects on \(W_n\), in the sparse regime these effects are asymptotically negligible. Moreover, the kernel is defined as,
\[
K_n: (x,y,z,z') \longmapsto \text{Ber}\left(f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}(\rho_n(x,y))\mathbf{1}\{z=z'\} + f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}(\rho_n(x,y))\mathbf{1}\{z\neq z'\}\right)
\]



In the sparse regime, nodes have a finite expected degree. Accordingly, we assume that the connection functions do not depend on \(n\), namely, \(f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)} = f_\mathrm{in}\) and \(f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)} = f_\mathrm{out}\), and that
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^d} f_\mathrm{in}(\|x\|_2) + f_\mathrm{out}(\|x\|_2)\mathrm{d}x < \infty
\]
The metric is Euclidean, which allows the finite graphs to be viewed as restrictions of a single infinite random graph \citep{last2018lectures}. In this regime, the main question is weak recovery. 
By monotonicity, there exists a critical value \(\lambda_c \in [0,\infty]\) separating the unsolvable and solvable phases. The first result is an impossibility criterion based on the auxiliary random connection model
\(H_{\lambda,g(\cdot),d}\) defined on the same PPP of intensity \(\lambda\) where two points at distance \(r\) are connected independently with probability \(g(r)\). Denoting by \(\theta(H_{\lambda,g(\cdot),d})\) the percolation probability, the probability that the connected component of a typical node is infinite. They prove that if \(\theta(H_{\lambda,f_\mathrm{in}-f_\mathrm{out},d})=0\) then weak recovery is impossible. In other words, if the informative part \(f_\mathrm{in}-f_\mathrm{out}\) does not generate an infinite connected component, then the community information cannot propagate at long range. In particular, for \(d=1\), weak recovery is impossible for every \(\lambda>0\).
To prove the lower bound, they introduce an auxiliary problem called Information Flow from Infinity.
%It asks whether one can estimate the label of a typical node better than random, given the graph, the node locations, and the true labels of nodes far away from it.
They prove that this problem is easier than community detection, so its impossibility already implies the impossibility of weak recovery \citep{abbe2021community}.
For \(d\ge 2\), this yields the explicit lower bound,
\[
\lambda \le \left(\int_{\mathbb R^d}\bigl(f_\mathrm{in}(\|x\|_2)-f_\mathrm{out}(\|x\|_2)\bigr)\,dx\right)^{-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{weak recovery is impossible}
\]

On the positive side, they introduce the Good Bad Grid (GBG) algorithm, its complexity is \(O(n^2)\). Although the graph is globally sparse, it remains locally dense because geometry creates many short loops and common neighbors. Hence, for two nearby nodes, the number of common neighbors carries information on whether they belong to the same community. The algorithm first compares nearby pairs using such local statistics, then partitions the space into small cells and propagates consistent decisions across the grid. Its analysis relies on a coupling with a dependent site percolation process. They prove that if \(d\ge 2\) then there exists \(\lambda_{upper}<\infty\) such that weak recovery is solvable for all \(\lambda\ge \lambda_{upper}\). Combined with the lower bound above, this implies
\(0<\lambda_c<\infty\) so the sparse regime exhibits a non-trivial phase transition \citep{abbe2021community}.
In some special cases, they obtain a sharp characterization of the threshold. If connection functions are hard thresholds, that is, if there exist \(R_\mathrm{in}, R_\mathrm{out} \geq 0\) such that,
\[
f_\mathrm{in}: r \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{\{r\le R_\mathrm{in}\}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f_\mathrm{out}: r \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{\{r\le R_\mathrm{out}\}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
R_\mathrm{in}\ge R_\mathrm{out}
\]
then weak recovery is solvable if and only if \(\theta(H_{\lambda,f_\mathrm{in}-f_\mathrm{out},d})>0\).
So, in this hard threshold case, the phase transition is fully characterized. More generally, they also show a weak form of asymptotic optimality for GBG, when the signal is strong enough, the fraction of correctly classified vertices can be made arbitrarily close to \(1\). In our notation, this means that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\lambda_0>0\) such that for all \(\lambda\ge \lambda_0\),
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P\!\left(
A_n(\hat{\sigma}^{GBG, \lambda}_n,\sigma^\star)\ge 1-\varepsilon
\right)=1
\]

% This does not imply that GBG is optimal for every fixed \(\lambda\), and the exact characterization of the weak recovery threshold remains open beyond these special cases \citep{abbe2021community}.

A related question, well studied in the SBM, is whether a graph with communities can be distinguished from a null model without community structure. In \citep{abbe2021community}, the authors formulate the same testing problem for the GSBM. The null model is a random connection model without communities, with the same spatial distribution of nodes and connection function \((f_\mathrm{in}+f_\mathrm{out})/2\). Hence, the null and alternative models have the same average degree. They prove that, as soon as \(f_\mathrm{in}\neq f_\mathrm{out}\) on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, the two induced probability measures are mutually singular. Therefore, the distinguishability problem is solvable with probability \(1-o_n(1)\) for every \(\lambda>0\), so it exhibits no phase transition. They construct an explicit test based on local triangle profiles. An interesting fact is that, in the sparse regime, one may detect the presence of communities while still being unable to recover a partition better than random. This is in contrast with the sparse SBM with two communities, where distinguishability and weak recovery coincide \citep{mossel2012stochasticblockmodelsreconstruction}.


In the logarithmic regime, the connection functions now depend on \(n\) and satisfy for \(C_\mathrm{in}>C_\mathrm{out}\ge 0\),
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^d} f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}(\|x\|_{T_n})\,dx = C_\mathrm{in}\log n
\qquad
\int_{\mathbb R^d} f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}(\|x\|_{T_n})\,dx = C_\mathrm{out}\log n
\]
This is also the natural scale of connectivity for geometric graphs, which explains why exact recovery is studied in this regime. However, connectivity is not a necessary condition for exact recovery in the GSBM since the node locations are observed, isolated or weakly connected vertices may still be classified from their geometric position relative to the others.
For the impossibility result, they introduce the genie estimator and the notion of a flip bad vertex, namely a vertex whose label is misclassified by the local likelihood test when all other labels are revealed. Writing \(N_n^{\mathrm{flip}}\) for the number of such vertices, they prove that if \(\mathbb E(N_n^{\mathrm{flip}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \infty\) and if long range correlations between flip bad events are negligible, then \(N_n^{\mathrm{flip}}>0\) with high probability. Therefore exact recovery is impossible \citep{abbe2021community}.
On the positive side, they adapt the GBG algorithm to this regime. The local pairwise tests become much more accurate, since the number of common neighbors is now of order \(\log n\), and the proof no longer relies on the existence of a giant good component. Instead, they show that when the signal is strong enough, every cell is good with high probability, which yields exact recovery \citep{abbe2021community}.

They then focus on a particular logarithmic model, defined by
\[
f_\mathrm{in}^{(n)}: r \mapsto a\,\mathbf 1_{\{r\le (\log n)^{1/d}\}}
\qquad
f_\mathrm{out}^{(n)}: r \mapsto b\,\mathbf 1_{\{r\le (\log n)^{1/d}\}}
\qquad 0\le b<a\le 1
\]
This should be viewed as a non trivial instance of the logarithmic regime. Denote by \(\nu_d\) the volume of the unit ball in \(\mathbb R^d\). In this case, they obtain an explicit necessary condition for exact recovery,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GSBM_log_impossibility}
    \lambda \nu_d\Bigl(1-\sqrt{ab}-\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}\Bigr)<1
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{exact recovery is impossible}
\end{equation}
They also prove that there exists \(C(a,b,d) \geq 0\) such that
\(\lambda \nu_d\Bigl(1-\sqrt{ab}-\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}\Bigr)>C(a,b,d)\) 
is sufficient for exact recovery by GBG. They conjecture that the lower bound is in fact sharp. This conjecture was later confirmed by \citep{gaudio2024exactcommunityrecoverygeometric}. The same sharp threshold also appears as a corollary of a more general model, GHCM, developed in \citep{gaudio2024exact}.

More recently, \citep{gaudio2025exactrecoverygeometricsbm} extended this sharp result. They consider a model where edges are only possible if they are in a visibility radius \(r(\log n)^{1/d}\) with \(r>0\), and assume mild regularity conditions on the connection functions such as support contained in \([0,r]\), a finite number of intersections between \(f_\mathrm{in}\) and \(f_\mathrm{out}\), and no interval on which the two functions remain arbitrarily close. This includes in particular the previous indicator case, but also broad classes of piecewise regular or continuous kernels. Their main result is a complete characterization of the exact recovery threshold through the information quantity
\[
I(f_\mathrm{in},f_\mathrm{out})
\coloneqq
\lambda \nu_d r^d
\int_0^r
\Bigl(
1-\sqrt{f_\mathrm{in}(t)f_\mathrm{out}(t)}
-\sqrt{(1-f_\mathrm{in}(t))(1-f_\mathrm{out}(t))}
\Bigr)
\frac{d\,t^{d-1}}{r^d}\,dt
\]
which is a generalized Chernoff Hellinger divergence. They prove that exact recovery is achievable when \(I(f_\mathrm{in},f_\mathrm{out})>1\), while it is impossible when \(I(f_\mathrm{in},f_\mathrm{out})<1\). In dimension \(d=1\), one must additionally have \(\lambda r>1\), and exact recovery is impossible when \(\lambda r<1\). Hence, the paper shows that the exact recovery problem in the two community GSBM admits a sharp information theoretic threshold far beyond the step function case. They also provide a polynomial time algorithm achieving this threshold.


\subsection{Geometric Kernel Block Model (GKBM)}

\citep{avrachenkov2024community} introduce the Geometric Kernel Block Model (GKBM) which extends the logarithmic two community geometric setting by replacing the hard threshold rule with a more general geometric kernel. The geometric positions are observed, so the space \(\mathcal X \coloneqq  \left]-\frac12,\frac12 \right ] \) is not latent, and it is equipped with the toroidal metric \(\rho\). Here again, \(n\) is a scaling parameter and not the number of nodes.
Points are sampled as a PPP on \(\mathcal{X}\) with intensity measure \(\mu_n \coloneqq \lambda n \mathrm{Leb}_{\mid \mathcal{X}}\) with \(\lambda > 0\). They focus in the one dimensional case with two symmetric communities, although the techniques may extend to higher dimensions. The link probabilities are defined according to \(\phi : \mathbb{R}^+ \to [0,1]\) measurable with a support bounded by \(r >0\). Let \(a,b \in [0,1]\),
\[
\mathrm{GKBM}(\lambda,a,b,\phi)\coloneqq
\mathrm{UGCM}(\mathcal X,\rho,\mathcal X,\mu_n,\{\pm1\},\mathrm{Ber}(1/2),\{0,1\},K_n)
\]
Moreover they study the logarithmic regime so the kernel is set as,
\[
K_n:(x,y,z,z')\longmapsto \mathrm{Ber}\!\left(\bigl(a\mathbf 1\{z=z'\}+b\mathbf 1\{z\neq z'\}\bigr)\phi\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\rho(x,y)\right)\right)
\]

The hard threshold version of the logarithmic GSBM leads to the explicit bound \eqref{eq:GSBM_log_impossibility}. For the GKBM, the authors replace this hard threshold by a more general kernel on their restricted one dimensional model, and introduce the information quantity
\[
I_\phi(a,b)\coloneqq 2\int_{\mathbb R_+}\left(1-\sqrt{ab}\,\phi(x)-\sqrt{(1-a\phi(x))(1-b\phi(x))}\right)\,dx
\]
This quantity plays the same role as in \citep{abbe2021community}. In fact, when \(\phi=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}\), it reduces to the one dimensional hard threshold case. Their main result shows that exact recovery is impossible if \(\lambda r<1\) or \(\lambda I_\phi(a,b)<1\). Conversely, if \(\lambda r>1\) and \(\lambda I_\phi(a,b)>1\), then exact recovery is achievable under the additional assumption that \(\phi(x)>0\) for all \(x\in[0,r]\) \citep{avrachenkov2024community}. They also propose a two phase algorithm, adapted from the recent GSBM exact recovery approach, where labels are first recovered on a small block and then propagated. The paper states that this procedure runs in time linear in the number of edges, this corresponds to a complexity of \(O(n\log n)\), in the logarithmic regime \citep{avrachenkov2024community}.


\subsection{Geometric Hidden Community Model (GHCM)}

The most general model in this line of work is the Geometric Hidden Community Model (GHCM), as formulated in \citep{gaudio2026exact}. The main generalization is that pairwise observations are no longer restricted to Bernoulli edges, so we take \(\mathcal Y \coloneqq \mathbb R\).
The geometric positions are observed, so the space \(\mathcal X \coloneq \mathbb{R}^d\) is not latent. Let \( W_n \coloneqq \left[-\frac{n^{1/d}}2,\frac{n^{1/d}}2\right]^d\) equipped with the toroidal metric \(\rho_n\). Let \(k\in\mathbb N\), \(Z\) be a label set with \(|Z|=k\) and prior \(\pi \coloneqq (\pi_i)_{i\in Z}\). Points are sampled as a PPP on \( W_n\) with intensity measure \(\mu_n \coloneqq \lambda \mathrm{Leb}_{\mid  W_n}\) with \(\lambda > 0\).
The most general GHCM model, from \citep{gaudio2026exact}, is distance dependent and is parametrized by a family of pairwise observation laws. For some \(r>0\), let
\[
P=\bigl(P_{zz'}(t)\bigr)_{z,z'\in Z,\ t\in[0,r]}\in \mathcal P(\mathcal Y)^{Z\times Z\times [0,r]}
\]
where \(P_{zz'}(t)\) is the law of the observation between two vertices of labels \(z\) and \(z'\) at distance \(t\). Since the graph is undirected, we assume \(P_{zz'}(t)=P_{z'z}(t)\). In the papers, these laws are taken either discrete or with density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb R\). We write \(p_{zz'}(\cdot;t)\) for a probability mass function or a density of \(P_{zz'}(t)\).
\[
\mathrm{GHCM}(P,\lambda,\pi,k,d,r) \coloneqq \mathrm{UGCM}(\mathcal X,\rho_n,W_n,\mu_n,Z,\pi,\mathcal Y,K_n)
\]
The kernel \(K_n\) is then given by
\[
K_n : (x,y,z,z') \longmapsto
\begin{cases}
P_{zz'}\!\left(\dfrac{\rho_n(x,y)}{(\log n)^{1/d}}\right) & \text{if } \rho_n(x,y)\le r(\log n)^{1/d}\\
\delta_0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
Thus geometry determines whether an observation is available and changes the law of this observation through the distance.
% This model definition allows us to capture problem such as \(\mathbb{Z}^2\)-synchronisation, submatrix localization and more.

The original GHCM of \citep{gaudio2024exact} is recovered when \(P_{zz'}(t)\) does not depend on \(t\), so geometry acts as an observation mask, it only determines whether this law is sampled or not. Hence the visibility radius is fixed to \((\log n)^{1/d}\), which corresponds to \(r=1\). This model contains the GSBM as the Bernoulli special case. The paper studies exact recovery in the logarithmic regime and identifies the information theoretic threshold,
\[
\lambda \nu_d \min_{z\neq z'} D_+(\theta_z,\theta_{z'})=1
\qquad \text{with} \qquad
\theta_z\coloneqq(p_{z1},\dots,p_{zk})
\]
with the additional one dimensional \(d=1\) obstruction, \(\lambda<1\). 
In the present discrete/continuous setting, \(D
_+\) is the Chernoff-Hellinger divergence given by
\[
D_+(\theta_z,\theta_{z'})
=
1-\inf_{t\in[0,1]}
\sum_{r\in Z}\pi_r \int_{ \mathcal{Y} } p_{zr}(y)^t p_{z'r}(y)^{1-t}\,dy
\]
with the integral replaced by a sum in the discrete case. Above the threshold, exact recovery is achieved by a polynomial time algorithm in two phases under two main technical assumptions. The first phase achieves almost exact recovery using MAP estimator to initialize propagation.
Then, the second phase refines the labeling and the model exhibits the same local to global amplification phenomenon as in the SBM \citep{abbe2018community}
Both assumptions are a bounded log-likelihood ratio, and a distinctness condition requiring that for every \(z\in Z\), the laws \(P_{zi}\) and \(P_{zj}\) are different whenever \(i\neq j\). The first one can however be relaxed in some special cases, in particular for Gaussian variants such as \(\mathbb Z_2\)-synchronization \citep{gaudio2024exact}.
The latter is used for the first phase during the propagation.
In dimension \(1\), it requires the additional condition \(\lambda>1\). This extra assumption only appears when the model still has non trivial permissible relabelings, that is, when the set
\[
\Omega_{\pi,P}\coloneqq \{\omega:Z\to Z \text{ permutation}, \forall z,z'\in Z \\pi_z=\pi_{\omega(z)} \text{ and } P_{zz'}=P_{\omega(z)\omega(z')} \}
\]
contains more than the identity. In the special case \(|\Omega_{\pi,P}|=1\), the paper instead uses the stronger assumption that all laws \(P_{zz'}\) are pairwise distinct, and then no longer needs \(\lambda>1\).


A subsequent work removes this distinctness assumption in a relevant two community setting. In \citep{gaudio2025sharpexactrecoverythreshold}, they consider \(k=2\), \(Z=\{1,2\}\), \(d\ge 2\), and observation laws satisfying \(P_{11}\neq P_{12}\) while \(P_{21}=P_{22}\).  
This asymmetric setting captures, for instance, geometric planted dense subgraph and geometric submatrix localization, where the goal is to identify one structured community against a homogeneous background. 
The information theoretic threshold is not changed. The difficulty is to build the first phase without distinctness. To resolve this, informally, the propagation of label is made by a clever and adaptive way. This yields almost exact recovery when \(\pi_1\lambda \nu_d>1\), and then exact recovery above the usual threshold \citep{gaudio2025sharpexactrecoverythreshold}. In the two community setting, the algorithm is robust to monotone semirandom perturbation, that's to say where an adversary is allowed to add intra community edges and delete inter community edges \citep{gaudio2024exact}.

The last step is done in \citep{gaudio2026exact}, where the GHCM becomes fully distance dependent as in this definition.
The information theoretic threshold then keeps the same form, but the Chernoff-Hellinger quantity must now average the information over all visible distances,
\[
\lambda \nu_d r^d \min_{z\neq z'} D_+(\theta_z,\theta_{z'};\pi,g)=1
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
g:t\mapsto \frac{d\,t^{d-1}}{r^d}\mathbf 1_{[0,r]}(t)
\]
where \(\theta_z(t)\coloneqq (p_{z1}(\cdot;t),\dots,p_{zk}(\cdot;t))\) and
\[
D_+(\theta_z,\theta_{z'};\pi,g)
=
1-\inf_{s\in[0,1]}
\sum_{\ell\in Z}\pi_\ell
\int_0^r
g(t)
\left(
\int_{\mathcal Y}
p_{z\ell}(y;t)^s p_{z'\ell}(y;t)^{1-s}\,dy
\right)\,dt
\]
with the inner integral over \(y\) replaced by a sum in the discrete case. The impossibility result is sharp below this threshold, with the additional one dimensional obstruction \(\lambda r<1\) when \(|\Omega_{\pi,P}|\ge 2\). Above the threshold, exact recovery is again achieved by a polynomial time two phase algorithm under similar assumptions to the constant probability case.



\section{Spectral methods in latent geometric models}

% Before turning to spectral methods designed specifically for geometric community models, we mention related works that clarify why geometry makes spectral recovery delicate.
In sparse SBM, non geometric model, \citep{bordenave2015non} showed that spectral recovery may depend on the spectral representation that is used, working with non-backtracking operator rather than the common used adjacency matrix. In geometric graphs another difficulty appears, dominant eigenvectors may reflect the ambient geometry rather than the latent communities.
\citep{meliot2019asymptotic} analyzed the spectrum of random geometric graphs, on compact Lie groups, illustrating that geometry alone can leave a strong signature in the spectrum even in the absence of communities.
A related but distinct perspective was developed by \citep{chen2016community}, who studied exact recovery from noisy pairwise measurements available only on a local observation graph, such as lines, rings, grids, or small-world networks, instead of being sampled over all node pairs. They proposed two nearly linear time algorithms, based on a spectral initialization followed by propagation and local refinement, and proved that these methods achieve the information theoretic threshold for a broad class of locality constrained graphs.
Then, \citep{peche2020robustness} proved that spectral recovery in the SBM can remain robust under suitable random geometric perturbations. They provide the first bridge between classical spectral community detection and geometric models.


Unlike the models discussed in Section 3, \citep{avrachenkov2021higher} is included here mainly for its spectral method rather than for a new information theoretic threshold. They introduce the Soft Geometric Block Model (SGBM), where the geometric positions are latent, so the space \(\mathcal X := \mathbb T^d = \mathbb R^d/\mathbb Z^d\) is not observed. The node positions are sampled i.i.d. uniformly on \(\mathcal X\) from \(\mu\). They study two balanced communities and define
\[
\mathrm{SGBM}(F_{\mathrm{in}},F_{\mathrm{out}},d)
\coloneqq
\mathrm{UGCM}\!\left(
\mathcal X,\rho,\mathcal X,\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal X),\{\pm1\},\mathrm{Ber}\!\left(\tfrac12\right),\{0,1\},K
\right)
\]
where \(\rho\) is the toroidal metric on \(\mathbb T^d\) and
\[
K : (x,y,z,z')
\longmapsto
\mathrm{Ber}\!\Big(
F_{\mathrm{in}}(x-y)\mathbf 1_{\{z=z'\}}
+
F_{\mathrm{out}}(x-y)\mathbf 1_{\{z\neq z'\}}
\Big)
\]
Here \(F_{\mathrm{in}},F_{\mathrm{out}}:\mathbb T^d\to[0,1]\) are measurable connection functions. This framework contains for instance the SBM, the GBM and more. 

\citep{avrachenkov2021higher} showed the Fiedler vector may correspond to a balanced geometric cut rather than to the hidden communities, so the informative eigenvector is not necessarily associated with the second eigenvalue. Rather than taking the second eigenvector, they identify the relevant eigenvalue from the Fourier coefficients of \(F_{\mathrm{in}}\)
and
\(F_{\mathrm{out}}\) and use the associated eigenvector for clustering.
In the dense regime, they characterize the limiting spectrum of the normalized adjacency matrix, they prove almost exact recovery for their method and then upgrade it to exact recovery by a refinement step.
Their assumptions on \(F_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{out}}\) are mild. They require a Fourier regularity condition at \(0\), which is satisfied for a wide class of reasonable kernels, including functions differentiable at \(0\) and piecewise \(C^1\) functions continuous at \(0\). The main limitation is that the analysis is restricted to the dense regime, so it does not address the sparse or logarithmic settings that are central in Section~\ref{sec:models}.


\citep{allem2025multicommunityspectralclusteringgeometric} extend the SGBM to consider \(k \geq 2\) homogeneous communities. Their goal is to identify a spectral procedure in a geometric setting with latent positions, not to find information theoretic thresholds. 
Writing
\[
\mu_{\mathrm{in}} := \int_{\mathbb T^d} F_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\,dx
\qquad
\mu_{\mathrm{out}} := \int_{\mathbb T^d} F_{\mathrm{out}}(x)\,dx
\]
they define the target value
\(\lambda^\star := n(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}-\mu_{\mathrm{out}}) / k\).
Their algorithm selects the \(k-1\) eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix whose eigenvalues are closest to \(\lambda^\star\), embeds the vertices into \(\mathbb R^{k-1}\) using the rows of the resulting matrix, and then applies \(k\)-means. The informative object is no longer a single eigenvector but a \((k-1)\)-dimensional eigenspace. In the dense regime from a Davis-Kahan perturbation argument, they prove almost exact recovery and then obtain exact recovery after a refinement step. This result is under the same assumptions as in the two-community case, namely \(F_{\mathrm{in}}(0)=\widehat F_{\mathrm{in}}(0)\), \(F_{\mathrm{out}}(0)=\widehat F_{\mathrm{out}}(0)\), and 
\[
\widehat F_{\mathrm{in}}(z)+(k-1)\widehat F_{\mathrm{out}}(z)\neq \mu_{\mathrm{in}}-\mu_{\mathrm{out}}
\quad \forall z\in\mathbb Z^d
\]
\[
\widehat F_{\mathrm{in}}(z)-\widehat F_{\mathrm{out}}(z)\neq \mu_{\mathrm{in}}-\mu_{\mathrm{out}}
\quad \forall z\in\mathbb Z^d\setminus\{0\}
\]

\section{Extensions and open directions}

The models reviewed in this report are mostly Euclidean and are designed for inference under explicit probabilistic assumptions on labels, positions, and edge formation. A different direction comes from hyperbolic latent representations. This viewpoint is motivated by the fact that hyperbolic geometry can reproduce several structural properties frequently observed in empirical networks, such as degree heterogeneity \citep{Baraba_si_1999}, strong clustering \citep{Krioukov_2016} and small-world \citep{watts1998collective}. 
In this setting, communities are not necessarily introduced as planted labels in a recovery problem, but may instead emerge from the geometric organization of the embedded graph.
This idea already appears in earlier hyperbolic embedding works such as HyperMap \citep{Papadopoulos_2015}, where the inferred hyperbolic coordinates reveal soft communities and can also be used for link prediction.

From this perspective, \citep{bruno2019community} study community detection after embedding an observed graph into a latent hyperbolic space using Mercator. Communities are then extracted from the angular organization of the embedded nodes through clustering heuristics. Their goal is not to establish information theoretic thresholds or recovery guarantees, but rather to use hyperbolic geometry as an intermediate representation in which community structure may become more detectable.
% This makes the approach conceptually closer to latent-space modeling and network representation than to the exact recovery results surveyed above.
A limitation of this purely geometric view is that geometry alone does not necessarily explain arbitrary mesoscale mixing patterns. This point is emphasized by \citep{guarino2025random}. They argue that classical hyperbolic models don't provide explicit control of block level interactions. To address this, they introduce a random hyperbolic block model, in which an explicit mesoscale structure is superimposed on the latent hyperbolic geometry. For the present survey, these works suggest that hyperbolic geometry is a promising representational framework, but not yet a theory of community recovery comparable to what is now available.

Returning to models reviewed above, an important open question is parameter estimation. In \citep{abbe2021community}, the authors discuss a version of the GBG procedure that does not require explicit knowledge of the connection functions. However, turning such ideas into a statistically principled estimation method remains open.
In particular, estimating $f_{\mathrm{in}}$, $f_{\mathrm{out}}$ or more generally the observation kernels, is poorly understood.
Likewise, many theoretical works assume that the geometric positions are observed, whereas in practice the geometry may be latent, noisy, or only partially available.

A second direction is to weaken assumptions. For instance, in the GHCM of \citep{gaudio2026exact},  sharp thresholds are now available in considerable generality, but the algorithmic results still rely on distinctness type assumptions ensuring that local neighborhoods contain enough information to separate the communities. Removing these assumptions, or replacing them by weaker %identifiable
conditions, remains an open problem.

A third direction concerns algorithms. On the one hand, higher-order spectral clustering is effective in the dense regime \citep{avrachenkov2021higher,allem2025multicommunityspectralclusteringgeometric}. On the other hand, the sharp exact recovery results in the logarithmic regime rely on local propagation followed by refinement \citep{galhotra2023communityrecoverygeometricblock,abbe2021community,avrachenkov2024community,gaudio2026exact}. A natural question is therefore whether one can design spectral or hybrid methods that remain effective in sparser geometric graphs.
