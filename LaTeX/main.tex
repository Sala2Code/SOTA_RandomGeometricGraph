\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Community detection consists in finding clusters in a graph where nodes share more connections with nodes of the same cluster
than with nodes of other clusters. This broad topic is detailed in \citep{fortunato2010community}. In this 
report, we focus on random geometric graphs (RGG). Adding spatial information allows us to consider
local information based on real or abstract proximity.  % The probability of connection between two nodes depends on their distance.
In social networks, two individuals are more likely to be friends if they are geographically close,
or if they share similar interests, age,\(\dots\)
More concrete examples of RGG can be found in wireless networks \citep{gomez2015case}, in biology \citep{higham2008fitting},
and also in community detection problems \citep{sankararaman2020comhapdet}
\textcolor{red}{ word embedding, physic, ...}.

The main reference model for community detection is the Stochastic Block Model (SBM)
\citep{holland1983stochastic}. Each node carries a hidden community label and edge probabilities depend on whether two nodes belong to the same community or not.
This model comes from the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph, where each node connects to others with the same probability \citep{erdHos1960evolution}. This model is now well understood,
see \citep{abbe2018community}. Nonetheless, it has some drawbacks, since it is unrealistic to think that every node can connect to every other one with the same probability scale.
Around the same time as Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi, the RGG was introduced by \citep{gilbert1961random}.
Add geometric information allows us to mimic some emergent behaviours of real networks,
such as sparsity \citep{Del_Genio_2011}, the "small-world" property \citep{watts1998collective}
or transitivity, namely the idea that a friend of a friend is more likely to be a friend.


RGG and its properties are well studied, see \citep{penrose2003random} and more recently \citep{duchemin2023random}.
When we add community structure, we are in a different setting.
We observe the graph, and sometimes also the node locations,
while the community labels remain hidden. 
The natural question is then whether the latent structure can be detected, and if so, how accurately the labels can be recovered.
We are interested in several levels of inference.
First, \textit{distinguishability} asks whether the observed graph comes from a geometric model with communities or from a null model without community structure. This is a testing problem. It aims to distinguish the presence of a community structure, not to recover the labels. Then, if recovery is possible, one can ask how accurate it can be.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Weak/partial recovery}: detecting communities better than random guessing
    \item \textit{Almost exact recovery}: the fraction of misclassified vertices vanishes as the number of nodes grows.
    \item \textit{Exact recovery}: all community labels are correctly recovered, up to permutation.
\end{itemize}
Thus, in this report, community detection is not only understood as
producing a convenient partition of the observed graph, but rather as
recovering latent community labels under a probabilistic geometric model.
% up to permutation.


Previously, we emphasized properties that RGG share with real networks due
to the geometric information. However,
one can also obtain transitivity, and surely more, with attribute-based models
\citep{Li_Sha_Huang_Zhang_2018}, 
hypergraphs \citep{Ruggeri_2023} and even geometric hypergraphs
\citep{Kov_cs_2025}.
We will not consider these models here, in order to keep the focus on the geometric aspect.
Moreover, among the problems mentioned above, one is \textit{Distinguishability} which should not be confused with the \textit{Detection Problem} that we do not study here.
The latter usually asks for conditions on the parameters defining the RGG under which the model is distinguishable from 
an Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graph. Hence, this is not directly a community question. See,
\citep{bubeck2016testing}
\citep{liu2022testing}
\citep{mao2026randomgeometricgraphssmooth}.
Finally, at the beginning of the literature,
some works were interested in recovering the latent position of nodes in the geometric space,
and considered this as a community detection problem, \citep{valdivia2019latent}
\citep{eldan2022community}.
This is a different problem from the one we are interested in,
and it's in some sense harder, because one wants to recover the position (a continuous label) of each node rather than a community (a discrete label).

\section{Notations, inference problems and models}

Formally, we observe a graph \(G \coloneqq (V,E)\), where \(V\) is the vertex set and \(E\) is the set of edges between vertices. Following results are asymptotics in \( n \coloneqq |V|\), the number of nodes. We consider a simple undirected graph \textcolor{red}{Une remarque là dessus ? Pourquoi non-orienté est classique comme hypothese ?}, without self-loops. The vertices are partitioned into \(k\) communities \(C_1,\dots,C_k\), so
\(V=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^k C_i\). We denote by \(\sigma^\star:V\to [k]\) the ground-truth community label function, defined by \(\sigma^\star(v)=i\) whenever \(v\in C_i\). The community structure is hidden, and our goal is to recover it from the observed data.

An estimator is therefore a function of the observations. Depending on the model, the observations may consist only of the graph \(G\), or of the graph together with the node locations \(X=(X_v)_{v\in V}\). Accordingly, we write the estimator as \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G)\) or \(\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}(G,X)\), and we view \(\hat{\sigma}:V\to [k]\) as an estimated labeling.
In general, the labels are only identifiable up to a relabeling of the communities. Indeed, permuting the names of the communities does not change the underlying partition of the vertex set. For this reason, the quality of an estimator is measured up to permutation. Denoting by \(\mathfrak{S}_k\) the symmetric group on \([k]\), we define
\[
A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)
\coloneqq \frac{1}{|V|}
\max_{\pi\in \mathfrak{S}_k}
\sum_{u\in V}
\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\hat{\sigma}(u)=\pi(\sigma^\star(u))\bigr\}
\]
Thus, \(A_n(\hat{\sigma},\sigma^\star)\) measures the fraction of correctly classified vertices after the best possible relabeling of the communities. 
We now give more formal definitions of the problems introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}.
\paragraph{Distinguishability.}
% This is a hypothesis testing problem introduced in \citep{abbe2021community}. Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of probability distributions corresponding to a geometric model with communities, and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\geq 1}\) be a sequence of null distributions without community structure. A test is a measurable map
% \(\varphi_n=\varphi_n(G,X)\in\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model. We say that \(\mathbb P_n\) and \(\mathbb Q_n\) are distinguishable if
% error of type I and type II errors tends to \(0\), i.e. there exists a sequence of tests
% \((\varphi_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that
% \[
% \mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \qquad\text{and}\qquad
% \mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
% \]
Following \citep{sankararaman2018community}, distinguishability is a hypothesis testing problem.
Let \((\mathbb P_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a geometric random graph model with community structure and let \((\mathbb Q_n)_{n\ge 1}\) be the laws of a null geometric model without communities, both defined
on the same observation space. A test is a measurable function
\(\varphi_n:\mathcal{X} \to\{0,1\}\) where \(\varphi_n=1\) means that we decide in favor of the community model.
We say that \((\mathbb P_n)\) and \((\mathbb Q_n)\) are distinguishable if there exist tests
\((\varphi_n)_{n\ge 1}\) and a constant \(\gamma>0\) such that, under the uniform prior on
\(\{\mathbb P_n,\mathbb Q_n\}\),
\[
\mathbb P\big(\text{\(\varphi_n\) makes the correct decision}\big)
= \frac12\,\mathbb P_n(\varphi_n=1)+\frac12\,\mathbb Q_n(\varphi_n=0)
\ge \frac12+\gamma-o_n(1)
\]

\paragraph{Weak/Partial recovery.}
We say that weak/partial recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) and a constant \(\alpha>1/k\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq \alpha\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Almost exact recovery.}
We say that almost exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that for every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)\geq 1-\varepsilon\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]
\paragraph{Exact recovery.}
We say that exact recovery is achievable if there exists a sequence of estimators \((\hat{\sigma}_n)_{n\geq 1}\) such that,
\[
\mathbb P\bigl(A_n(\hat{\sigma}_n,\sigma^\star)=1\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 1
\]

Most works in the literature focus on the case \(k=2\). Although several ideas extend beyond this setting, this is also the framework that we adopt in the following, except specify otherwise. In that case, it is convenient to encode the labels by \(\{-1,+1\}\), instead of \([2] = \{1,2\}\). Then “up to permutation” reduces to a global sign flip.

Each vertex also belongs to a space \(\mathcal X\). In the following, we specify each time whether this space is latent or observed. For theoretical analysis, \(\mathcal{X}\) is often chosen for mathematical convenience. The Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is a natural reference because of its symmetry, homogeneity, isotropy, and direct geometric interpretation. See \citep{abbe2021community}, \textcolor{red}{Citer ceux qui sont euclidiens}
We also encounter the torus, mainly to avoid boundary effects and to focus on the core ideas of the proofs, \textcolor{red}{Todo citer}. Lastly, for latent models, the high-dimensional unit sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d}\) is a standard reference space to model similarity, and it also provides convenient spectral properties \citep{decastro2020adaptiveestimationnonparametricgeometric}. A summary of theoretical works on \(\mathbb{S}^d\) is given in \citep{duchemin2023random}.
